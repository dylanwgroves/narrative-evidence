About

Methods and roadmap

What is included

Randomized experiments that test a narrative or entertainment-based intervention against a comparison condition and report an outcome we can standardize. The published meta-analysis covered 58 studies run between 2008 and 2020 in 13 countries, coding 398 separate findings. Since publication we have added 35 studies (mostly 2021–2026, from a repeat of the search in OpenAlex and the reference list of Peterman 2025, plus a forward-citation sweep), bringing the database to 93 studies in 26 countries and 663 findings on attitudes, beliefs, intentions, priorities, norms and behavior.

How the home page answers “will it work?”

  • What counts. By default, stories on their own compared with nothing or with unrelated content, one estimate per study. Stories compared with a plain information message answer a different question, so they are under More options.
  • Chance of a positive effect. Our estimate of the chance that a new program like these has a true effect above zero. It uses the typical effect, how much studies differ from each other, and the uncertainty in both (a prediction distribution, Higgins et al. 2009, t with k−2 degrees of freedom). It is not the chance that an effect is large. We show it only with at least five studies.
  • Effect size. d is the difference between groups in standard deviations. An effect of 0.2 puts the average person reached ahead of 58% of people not reached. Small effects can still matter when a program reaches many people cheaply.
  • Most relevant studies. Ranked by how many of your answers a study matches. Region and topic count most, then form and what you want to change. Ties favor real-world studies and larger samples. Studies that miss one answer can still appear, and say so.
  • Ask a question. AI (Claude, from Anthropic) turns your question into filters and a comparison. The site then calculates the charts and numbers itself, and the AI writes a short summary from those numbers only. Check the filters it chose.
  • Comparing groups of studies. Compare shows how groups differ, but studies were not randomly assigned to radio or TV, to one region or another, and so on. These comparisons are non-experimental. The table under each chart shows other measured differences that could explain a gap, and unmeasured ones can matter too.
  • Read with care. Published studies may overstate effects, many outcomes are self-reported, and most are measured soon after exposure. Themes, forms and summaries are a first pass from abstracts and papers and have not yet been checked by a second coder.

How the effects are pooled here

  • One per study averages a study's findings before pooling so studies with many outcomes do not dominate. Every finding treats each finding as independent.
  • Random-effects estimates between-study variance with DerSimonian–Laird; fixed-effect uses inverse-variance weights only.
  • Heterogeneity is summarized with I² and τ²; Egger's regression gives a rough check for small-study bias.
Exploration, not inference. The browser pools are transparent approximations. The published estimates use robust variance estimation (robumeta, hierarchical weights clustered on study, small-sample correction). Cite the article's models for inference.

Paper links

Each study was matched to its published record in OpenAlex. Matched papers link to their DOI, with a free full-text link where OpenAlex lists one. Studies without a reliable match, mostly theses and conference papers, link to a Google Scholar title search instead.
